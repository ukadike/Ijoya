# Ìjóyà: Borrowed Function in the Garden

## Canonical Project Logic

**Project:** Ìjóyà: Borrowed Function in the Garden\
**Practice:** Small Systems Lab\
**Artist:** Adekemi "Kemi" Sijuwade-Ukadike\
**Status:** Canonical project logic / working specification\
**Year:** 2026

> **We dance in the garden together because we cannot function alone.**

## 1. Core Proposition

Ìjóyà is a site-responsive sculptural system about interdependence.

It is not a collection of autonomous robots that happen to be connected.
**It is one distributed body expressed through multiple incomplete
bodies.**

Each sculptural body has capacities and absences. No individual body
contains every function required to operate independently. Function
emerges through relationship: bodies borrow balance, sensing, movement,
communication, tension, stability, orientation, and support from one
another.

The resulting collective movement is the dance.

## 2. Individual Incompleteness

Each body is intentionally incomplete.

One body may sense but not move effectively. Another may provide
structural stability but depend upon another body for environmental
information. Another may communicate through vibration or light while
borrowing balance from a neighboring form.

These differences are not defects to be corrected. They are the
conditions that make relationship necessary.

The work therefore rejects the assumption that independence is the
highest form of successful function.

## 3. Borrowed Function

**Borrowed function** is the central operating principle.

A capacity can exist across bodies rather than inside a single body. One
sculpture may perform part of the functional labor of another.

Borrowed functions may include:

-   balance;
-   structural support;
-   movement;
-   tension;
-   orientation;
-   sensing;
-   signaling;
-   vibration;
-   environmental awareness;
-   tactile communication; and
-   stabilization.

A body's capability is therefore relational rather than individually
owned.

## 4. One Distributed Body

The installation operates as a network.

A change experienced by one body can travel through a physical or
electronic connection and alter the condition of another. Pressure here
may create vibration there. Wind moving one form may change tension in
another. Touch at one node may produce light, movement, or tactile
response elsewhere.

This distributed consequence is essential.

The sculptures do not merely symbolize interdependence. **Their behavior
should depend upon it.**

## 5. The Dance

The "dance" in Ìjóyà is not choreography imposed upon independent
machines.

It is emergent collective movement produced by:

-   shared tension;
-   shifting weight;
-   wind;
-   touch;
-   environmental conditions;
-   flexible connections;
-   mechanical response;
-   soft-robotic actuation where necessary; and
-   the changing relationships among bodies.

A body moves because something else moved, supported it, touched it,
pulled it, sensed something, or changed the conditions around it.

The dance is the visible evidence of relationship.

## 6. Disability and Interdependence

Ìjóyà grows from disability experience without treating disability as
spectacle, deficit, medical illustration, or heroic overcoming.

A brace can perform part of the labor of a muscle. A cane changes the
relationship between a body and the ground. A wheelchair redistributes
movement through wheels, hands, surfaces, architecture, and technology.
Another person can temporarily provide balance or orientation.

Function already moves between bodies, objects, environments, and
systems.

Ìjóyà makes that reality sculptural.

Different capacities create the collective system rather than excluding
a body from it.

## 7. Accessibility as Communication Architecture

Accessibility is not an accommodation added after the artwork is
designed.

It is part of the communication system of the work.

Possible modalities include:

-   touch;
-   pressure;
-   vibration;
-   tension;
-   movement;
-   light;
-   sound where appropriate;
-   distinct silhouettes;
-   multiple viewing heights;
-   tactile interpretation;
-   audio description;
-   large-print interpretation; and
-   accessible placement in relation to pathways and seated sightlines.

No single sensory modality should be required to understand the central
relationship of the installation.

## 8. Cowrie Nodes

Cowrie forms operate as functional and cultural interface elements.

They are **not decorative appliqué**.

Depending on location and function, a cowrie node may act as:

-   an eye or sensing metaphor;
-   an illuminated signal;
-   a tactile landmark;
-   a touch interface;
-   a connection point between bodies;
-   a cover or diffuser for an LED;
-   a marker of active communication; or
-   a visible indication that function is passing between bodies.

Their meaning should remain legible at sculptural scale.

## 9. Environmental Participation

The garden is not a backdrop.

Wave Hill's environment becomes another participant in the distributed
body.

Relevant inputs may include:

-   wind;
-   sunlight and shadow;
-   temperature;
-   moisture;
-   plant movement;
-   ground conditions;
-   spatial transitions;
-   existing plant-support systems;
-   pathways;
-   visitor movement; and
-   seasonal change.

Environmental observation informs placement, orientation, connection,
movement, and material decisions.

The installation therefore responds to the particular conditions of the
garden rather than being a sculpture that could be placed unchanged
anywhere.

## 10. Human Relationship

Visitors encounter a network of bodies rather than a display of
autonomous robots.

Where interaction is appropriate, a visitor may touch, press, approach,
listen, feel vibration, observe transmitted movement, or otherwise
communicate with the system.

The visitor should be able to perceive that an action affecting one part
of the installation may have consequences elsewhere.

Interaction is therefore relational rather than command-based.

The work does not ask, "How can I control this robot?"

It asks, **"What changes elsewhere when I enter this relationship?"**

## 11. Scale

The current drawings are **concept and maquette studies**. They
establish body vocabulary, relationships, accessibility concepts,
connection logic, and possible technical systems.

They do **not** represent the intended final physical scale.

For Wave Hill, the sculptural bodies are envisioned at **human scale to
larger-than-human scale**, allowing visitors to encounter them spatially
and bodily within the garden.

Scaling up should also allow the forms to evolve beyond the visual
language of small dolls or toy robots.

The final bodies may become:

-   softer;
-   more abstract;
-   more sculptural;
-   more botanical;
-   more materially expressive;
-   more integrated with the landscape; and
-   less conventionally humanoid.

The concept is preserved by the relationships among bodies, not by
reproducing the exact proportions of the preliminary drawings.

## 12. Material and Technical Logic

Likely material systems include:

-   weather-resistant textile or mesh skins;
-   soft stuffed or inflated volumes;
-   flexible armatures;
-   braided lines or cords;
-   articulated or compliant joints;
-   removable mechanical connections;
-   tactile surfaces;
-   protected low-voltage electronics;
-   sensors;
-   vibration elements;
-   LEDs or diffused illumination; and
-   soft-robotic or mechanical actuation only where functionally
    necessary.

Technology remains subordinate to the sculptural relationship.

A sensor, motor, microcontroller, light, or actuator should exist only
when it helps make interdependence physically perceptible.

The project does not require artificial intelligence or autonomous
decision-making to achieve its central behavior.

## 13. System Logic

The basic system can be expressed as:

**ENVIRONMENT / VISITOR**\
↓\
**BODY A SENSES OR RECEIVES**\
↓\
**PHYSICAL / TACTILE / ELECTRONIC CONNECTION**\
↓\
**BODY B RESPONDS**\
↓\
**TENSION / MOVEMENT / SIGNAL PROPAGATES**\
↓\
**COLLECTIVE CONFIGURATION CHANGES**\
↓\
**THE DANCE EMERGES**

No response needs to be identical every time. Variation can arise
mechanically from material, weather, position, tension, and human
interaction without requiring opaque computation.

## 14. Design Test

Every major design decision should be tested against the following
question:

> **Does this make interdependence physically perceptible, or is it
> merely adding technology or decoration?**

If an element does not strengthen the relationship among bodies, garden,
and visitors, it should be reconsidered.

## 15. What Ìjóyà Is Not

Ìjóyà is not:

-   a collection of cute robots placed outdoors;
-   a demonstration of technological autonomy;
-   a disability simulation;
-   a metaphor in which connection exists only in explanatory text;
-   an AI installation;
-   a celebration of independence;
-   a garden decorated with electronic sculptures; or
-   a claim that every body must perform the same functions.

Its subject is **distributed capacity**.

## 16. Canonical Statement

> **Ìjóyà: Borrowed Function in the Garden is one distributed body
> expressed through multiple incomplete bodies. Each form has capacities
> and absences, borrowing movement, balance, sensing, communication,
> tension, and support from others. Through soft materials, tactile
> communication, environmental response, and selective soft robotics,
> consequences travel across the group. Wind, plants, visitors,
> pathways, and weather become participants in the system. Accessibility
> is not added to the installation; it is the architecture through which
> the bodies communicate. Their collective movement is the dance: we
> dance in the garden together because we cannot function alone.**

## 17. Canonical Rule

**The relationship is the artwork.**

The bodies make that relationship visible, tactile, spatial, and alive.
